# The Agalma

Watch someone scroll through Instagram at 2am, adjusting the angle in their reflection. That's prayer. Watch someone check their portfolio three times before breakfast. That's worship. Everyone has an altar. The only question is what's on it.

You don't get to choose whether you worship. You choose what. And what you choose determines how you suffer.

> "In the day-to-day trenches of adult life, there is actually no such thing as atheism. There is no such thing as not worshipping. Everybody worships. The only choice we get is what to worship."
>
> — David Foster Wallace, _This Is Water_

Your default setting is whatever your days organize around, the metric you reach for when you decide whether today counted. That's the altar. Almost nobody picks it deliberately. It arrives from culture, from parents, from the path of least resistance.

- Worship beauty, and you'll die a death by a thousand cuts. Checking your reflection in every storefront window, every phone screen, every car door. Adjusting the angle. Finding the light. Every birthday a defeat, every photo a disappointment.
- Worship intelligence, and you'll spend your life in the gap between what you know and what you think you should know. Every conversation becomes a test you're failing. Every silence, proof you're not enough.
- Worship power, and you'll never stop feeling powerless. Someone's always stronger, always higher, always holding cards you'll never see. The vulnerability doesn't go away. It multiplies.
- Worship money, and the treadmill speeds up with every milestone. The number that would have been enough five years ago is laughable now. The finish line was never there.

## Two kinds of suffering

You will suffer either way. The only question is which kind.

Misalignment suffering comes from external worship. You can do everything right and still lose, because outcomes are only partly tied to effort. Genetics, markets, timing, luck. All of it sits outside your control.

Meaningful fatigue comes from the other direction. The pain is in the difficulty of the work itself rather than in chasing a result you can't control. Did you show up today? Did you do the work? Did you serve the thing that matters? Those are questions you can actually answer.

You're tired because you built something. Spent because you gave everything to something worth giving to. The exhaustion has a shape.

Both compound. Misalignment suffering compounds into anxiety: more success, more to lose, more to defend. Meaningful fatigue compounds into capacity. More work, more range, more clarity about what matters.

Find something internal worth suffering for.

## What the Greeks meant

The Greeks had a word for something precious hidden inside something ordinary. _Agalma_ (ἄγαλμα) started out meaning a statue or a votive offering, a thing that pleases the gods. The philosophers moved it somewhere more interesting: the treasure buried in a person that makes them worth knowing.

<img src="/images/blog/greek-agalma.webp" alt="Ancient Greek statue of Silenus - unassuming exterior hiding sacred treasure within" loading="lazy" class="post-image-float-right" />

Greek statues were marble shells that once held something sacred. The word drifted from "that which pleases the gods" to "the thing of value hidden inside." Plato used it in the _Symposium_ when Alcibiades compared Socrates to the ugly Silenus figurines that crack open to reveal gold.

Gustave Doré is the clearest case I know of someone who found his and honored it completely. His story runs through the rest of this.

## Revealed through doing

Outside-in strategies chase external proof of worth: accumulate beauty, intelligence, power, money, and hope the feeling of value follows. Inside-out reverses it. You discover what matters through committed action and attention to internal signal. Outside-in asks what will make others think you're valuable. Inside-out asks what work feels true regardless of who's watching.

People want to know whether you discover the agalma or create it. Neither, quite. It's latent, like an image on undeveloped film. You create the conditions and it develops. The doing is what makes it visible.

Which sets up a real problem. You can't know what you're looking for until you find it, and you can't find it without committing to something first. How do you commit when you don't know what you're committing to?

You don't commit to the thing. You commit to the process of discovery.

So start with a constraint. Pick one direction, and it barely matters which as long as it genuinely pulls you forward, then commit to ninety days of consistent output. Not to find your agalma. You won't, not that fast. To generate signal. Build something, ship something, finish something. The constraint is the instrument, not the goal.

Paying attention means something specific here. After each piece of work: would you do this again if nobody paid you? Did it drain you in the right way or the wrong way? Which part of it keeps calling you back when you think about what's next? Write the answers somewhere you'll actually look again. The pattern comes from repetition rather than revelation, and you're watching for the thread that survives across attempts.

After ninety days, review the body of work. Not individual pieces. The collection. Which of it felt like novelty, exciting because new, and which felt like signal? Pick the strongest signal and run another cycle.

Novelty and signal feel different once you know to check. Novelty feels like excitement and fades when the newness does. Signal feels like recognition, and it stays when the work gets hard, when the external rewards disappear, when you've done it enough times that it should be boring and somehow isn't.

Doré didn't wake up at fifteen knowing his treasure was visualizing the divine at scale. He started by drawing everything. Landscapes, portraits, caricatures, street scenes. He took commissions he didn't want. He drew for newspapers, magazines, books, the commercial work the art establishment despised.

Somewhere in those decades a pattern emerged. What kept calling him back wasn't the prestigious portraits or the paying caricatures. It was the sacred narratives. The biblical scenes, the mythological epics, the work where he could render light breaking through darkness and the geometry of heaven and hell.

He didn't plan it. He discovered it by making thousands of illustrations and noticing which ones felt different. Which would he have done for nothing? Which images haunted him when he wasn't drawing? Which ones drained him in the right way?

The common mistake is trying to find your agalma by looking inward at achievements, status, and what other people think you should want. It reveals itself instead through what you do when the applause stops.

> The agalma is what you'd build in the dark.

## Doré

Gustave Doré (1832-1883) made more than 10,000 illustrations, and not by grinding alone in a studio. He built workshops of engravers who could replicate his art while he moved to the next thing.

He would create the master drawing in pen and ink, then hand it to engravers who transferred the image onto copper plates, reproducing every line and shadow. While they worked on production he was already somewhere else. The system let him keep artistic control at a scale no solo artist could reach. Most serious artists of his era read this as selling out. Doré read it as the only way to reach everyone who needed to see the work.

He shaped how millions of people picture the divine. His illustrations for Paradise Lost, Dante's Divine Comedy, and the Bible became the definitive imagery for angels, demons, and revelation in Western culture.

> _When you picture hell or heaven, you're seeing Doré's vision._

He could have chased what the art world valued: oil paintings, gallery exhibitions, critical prestige. That was the path to being called serious. He chose illustration, considered commercial and lowbrow, mere craft. He faced contempt from the establishment his entire life and Paris never gave him the recognition he wanted. He kept drawing, kept building his workshops, kept pushing his vision of the divine out at a scale no serious artist could touch.

His agalma was prolific contribution rather than perfection. The work mattered more than his ego, reaching millions mattered more than impressing critics, and moving truth at scale takes systems as much as craft. His particular treasure existed because the world needed to see these stories brought to life and he was willing to trade prestige to make it happen.

## Testing it

Finding your agalma is the easy part. The hard part is testing whether you found it or whether you confused passion with purpose. Three tests, and they're all uncomfortable.

The first is anonymity. Would you do this work if no one would ever know you did it? No signature, no credit, no proof it was yours. People fail this one quietly. They say credit doesn't matter, then something crosses their face when someone else gets recognition for similar work. The resentment is the answer. The work stops when the spotlight moves. Most of Doré's illustrations that reached millions carried other hands' marks on the plate, and he cared about dissemination more than attribution.

The second is impact. Is the world better because you did this? Not whether it makes you feel good, not whether people are impressed. The trap is confusing activity with contribution: busy, working hard, nothing moving. Doré's illustrations became how millions of people picture Paradise Lost, the Divine Comedy, the biblical scenes. He built a culture's visual vocabulary for heaven and hell. Most of us won't work at that scale. The question still applies at any scale.

The third is whether skill and passion actually meet. Passion without skill is delusion, the tone-deaf audition, the person convinced they were meant to perform. Loving something doesn't make it yours to do, and enjoying it casually at zero stakes tells you nothing. Doré made ten thousand illustrations of real technical merit. The passion drove the work and the skill is what made the work matter.

Past the tests, the questions change. Does this project serve the agalma or your ego? Are you disseminating truth or managing your image? If you can't answer cleanly, either you haven't found it or you've found it and you aren't honoring it. Both get the same response. Return to the work.

## What it isn't

It isn't follow your passion. Passion is a feeling and feelings rotate. Photography this year, woodworking next, pottery after that. That's novelty. The agalma is what you keep returning to once the passion has drained off and it's just work again. The real test arrives when it costs you and nobody's watching.

It isn't find your purpose either. Purpose implies a destiny sitting somewhere waiting, like you're a lock and there's one key that fits. You're a pattern that emerges through action. And being good at something is no argument for dedicating your life to it. Plenty of people are skilled at work that means nothing to them.

And it isn't do what makes you happy. Happiness is the wrong instrument. The agalma often makes you miserable. It costs you. It asks for things you'd rather keep. Doré stayed unhappy about the art world's contempt his whole life and kept drawing biblical scenes anyway, because the work outranked his comfort. If you'll only do what feels good, you have a hobby.

The prestige trap is the subtlest version of all three. You chase what looks impressive instead of what matters. External worship wearing the mask of internal purpose.

The agalma is what you serve when it costs you, when the passion has drained away and all that's left is the conviction that the work matters.

## How long

There's no timeline. Some people find the pattern in months and some take decades. Doré drew commercially for years before the sacred narratives separated themselves out from everything else.

The agalma doesn't require you to quit your job tomorrow. The day job is the subsidy that buys you enough attempts to see the shape.

What do you keep returning to after failures? When a project dies, which piece do you want to salvage? When everything's burning, what do you carry out?

There's a difference between not having found it yet and being in the wrong place. Not found yet looks like varied work, attention paid, nothing crystallized, data accumulating. Wrong place looks like repetition without attention: the same thing over and over, optimizing for external validation and calling it persistence. If you've been doing the same work for years and you're still asking whether this is it, figure out which one you're in.

## The obligation

When Doré found his, he found more than a thing he was good at. He found that sacred narratives could reach people at scale through illustration, and that truth wasn't his to hoard.

If you've found something true, you owe it outward. Real truth doesn't belong to you. You're the vessel.

Plato understood this in the cave allegory. Once you've climbed out and seen the sun, you have an obligation to those still in the dark. Not to drag them out against their will, but to make the path visible for the ones already searching. The hard part comes after the climbing. In Plato's version the philosopher returns to the cave to free the prisoners and they don't want freeing. They mock him, call him crazy, threaten to kill him for disturbing their comfortable illusions.

You're not obligated to go back and argue with the prisoners. You're not responsible for convincing people committed to their chains or hauling them toward a truth they aren't ready for. But some prisoners are already turned toward the light, already sensing something's wrong, already ready to climb. You are obligated to make the truth findable for them.

Dissemination is leaving breadcrumbs for people already hungry. It's a signal fire, lit so the ones searching in the dark know they aren't alone. Your particular treasure exists because someone needs to see it. Not everyone. Someone.

There are three ways to handle that and only one of them works.

You can hoard. Keep the work private, wait until it's perfect, tell yourself it isn't ready. Years pass and it still isn't ready. That fails the obligation outright, something true locked away from the people who need to find it.

You can signal. Share the work, but for status. You care more about the act of sharing, look at me, look what I made, than about whether it reaches anyone who needed it, so you post where the validation is instead of where the work is findable. That fails the anonymity test. The sharing is about you.

Or you can actually disseminate. Make the work findable for people already searching. Trade prestige for reach. Let it spread on terms you don't control, let others build on it, remix it, take credit for it, if that's what puts it in front of more of the people who need it.

Doré worked in the third mode. He could have chased gallery exhibitions and kept tight control of his originals. He took scale over attribution.

The question underneath all of it: what are you willing to give up to increase the work's reach? Working in a lowbrow medium. Letting others replicate you without your control. Taking scale over perfection. What you'll sacrifice is what separates a signal fire from a performance.

The dissemination is the whole point.

The Greeks understood something we've mostly forgotten. The precious thing isn't always visible from the outside. Sometimes it's buried in the broken, the weird, the unexpected. Sometimes it's in you, waiting on what you build to bring it out.

---

The agalma changes what the suffering is for. Hollow exhaustion becomes meaningful fatigue. Chasing validation you can't control becomes serving something that holds regardless of applause.

You can't think your way there. Pick the direction that keeps calling you back and put ninety days into it. Pay attention to what's left when the validation disappears, what you protect when the rest of it burns, what you return to after it fails.

I'm somewhere in the middle of my own cycles on this. The pattern is clearer than it was five years ago, and I don't expect it to finish resolving.
